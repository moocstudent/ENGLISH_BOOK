## Core Notes

### Rhotic vs non-rhotic

The clearest BrE/AmE split: whether written **r** is pronounced at the end of a word or before a consonant.

- **Rhotic (r sounded):** AmE, Scottish, Irish. *car* /kɑːr/, *hard* /hɑːrd/.
- **Non-rhotic (r dropped):** BrE (RP), AusE, NZ. *car* /kɑː/, *hard* /hɑːd/.
- The r **returns when linking:** *car alarm* → "ca-r-alarm".

Hear a clear final -r → probably American; no -r → probably British or Australian.

### Vowel differences

| Word | BrE | AmE | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| bath, dance, can't | /ɑː/ | /æ/ | the BATH split |
| lot, hot, dog | /ɒ/ | /ɑː/ | rounded vs not |
| go, home | /əʊ/ | /oʊ/ | diphthong start |
| tomato | /təˈmɑːtəʊ/ | /təˈmeɪtoʊ/ | classic split |

**AmE flapping (key for listening):** /t/ between vowels becomes a soft /d/ — *water* → "wadder", *better* → "bedder", *city* → "ciddy".

**AusE glimpse:** *day* sounds close to "die"; statements often rise at the end like questions.

### Information layers in fast speech

English is **stress-timed**: stressed syllables fall at roughly even intervals; unstressed ones squeeze in and reduce.

- **Content words** (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, wh-words) carry stress = the **information**.
- **Function words** are weak = grammar glue.

**Ride the stress:** catch the stressed content words and you get about 90% of the meaning — *I would've GONE to the PARTY but I was TIRED.*

### Adaptation strategies

- **Master one accent, then add others** — hours of one accent tune your ear before you rotate.
- **Multi-accent diet:** a week of AmE, a week of BrE, and so on.
- **Drop "understand every word":** aim for the message; even natives miss words and fill from context.
- Tolerate ambiguity — predict from context instead of freezing on one unclear word.

### Summary

Rhotic sounds r, non-rhotic drops it. Vowels differ and AmE flaps /t/ to /d/. Ride the stress; master one accent, then rotate.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1.** You hear *water* as "wadder", *better* as "bedder". → **AmE flapping** (/t/ → /d/ between vowels).

**Example 2.** A speaker sounds every final -r in *car, hard, mother*. → **rhotic** accent, likely American.

**Example 3.** Stressed words in "I've been WORKing on this REPORT ALL WEEK"? → **working / report / all week** (catch these; *I've been* is weak).

**Example 4.** *tomato* in BrE vs AmE? → **/təˈmɑːtəʊ/** vs **/təˈmeɪtoʊ/**.

**Example 5.** Multi-accent plan: main accent this week / minutes per day / second accent added / one feature you noticed.

## Exercises

1. Rhotic vs non-rhotic — define and name one accent each.
2. What happens to /t/ in AmE *little*? Give a similar word.
3. Why does catching content words give you the meaning?
4. When does a non-rhotic /r/ come back?
5. Why not aim to understand every word of a new accent?
6. Sketch your own one-week multi-accent plan.

**Answer key.**

1) Rhotic sounds all r (AmE); non-rhotic sounds r only before a vowel (BrE, AusE). 2) It flaps to a soft /d/ (as in *water*, *city*). 3) Content words carry stress and information; catching them gives about 90% of meaning. 4) In linking, before a following vowel (*far away*). 5) Aim for the message; context fills gaps and natives miss words too. 6) Self-check.
