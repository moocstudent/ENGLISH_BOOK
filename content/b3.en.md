## Passage

*— Adapted from The Adventures of Tom Sawyer, Mark Twain (1876). A public-domain work.*

Saturday morning came bright and fresh, but Tom Sawyer felt no joy. Aunt Polly had set him to whitewash the long board fence — thirty yards of it, high above his head. He dipped his brush, painted one small stripe, and looked at the endless grey boards with a sinking heart.

Then a wonderful idea came to him. When Ben Rogers strolled by, munching an apple and pretending to be a steamboat, Tom went on painting and took no notice. Ben stopped and laughed. "You've got to work, hey?" Tom turned as if surprised. Work? Well, perhaps it was and perhaps it wasn't; all he knew was that it suited Tom Sawyer very well. Did a boy get the chance to whitewash a fence every day?

Now the fence looked like a rare treasure. Tom swept his brush to and fro, stepped back to study the effect, and added a careful touch here and there, like an artist. Ben watched, more and more interested, until he could bear it no longer and begged for a turn. At first Tom refused — Aunt Polly was so particular about this fence. Only at last, and for the price of the apple, did he hand over the brush.

By afternoon boy after boy had come to jeer and stayed to paint. Each one paid for the privilege, and Tom, resting in the shade, had grown rich in marbles and small treasures, while the fence wore three thick coats of white.

## Notes

- **Source / author:** *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer* (1876) is a classic boyhood novel by the American writer Mark Twain. This passage is an adaptation of its famous "whitewashing" chapter, not the original text.
- **Main idea:** Tom disguises a dull chore as a rare treat and plays on the other boys' curiosity and pride, so that they beg to do his work — and pay him for it. The humour reveals something true about human nature.
- **Structure / craft:** It moves from Tom's gloom to his clever idea; his performance of "enjoying" the job and Ben's change of heart create a comic reversal, ending with Tom "rich."
- **Tone:** Light and humorous, with gentle irony and boyish cunning.

## Reading Comprehension

1. What is the main point of the passage?
- A) Tom truly enjoys hard work more than play.
- B) Tom tricks other boys into doing his chore and even paying for it.
- C) Tom is punished and never finishes the fence.
- D) Tom teaches the other boys how to paint properly.

2. Why was Tom unhappy at the start of the passage?
- A) He had to whitewash a long, high fence as a punishment.
- B) His friends had refused to play with him.
- C) He had lost his paintbrush.
- D) Aunt Polly had taken away his apple.

3. Why does Tom pretend to refuse when Ben asks for a turn?
- A) He truly does not want any help.
- B) He wants to finish the whole fence himself.
- C) He is afraid Ben will paint the fence badly.
- D) He wants to make the job seem special so Ben will want it even more.

4. In the passage, "particular about this fence" means Aunt Polly was
- A) planning to take it down soon
- B) paying a great deal of money for it
- C) fussy and hard to please about it
- D) worried that it would fall over

5. The tone of the passage can best be described as
- A) light and humorous
- B) tense and frightening
- C) sad and serious
- D) angry and bitter

**Answer Key**

1) **B** — Main idea. Tom makes the chore look like a treat, the other boys "paid for the privilege" to paint, and he ends up resting and "grown rich." He does not really love work, the fence gets three coats, and he is not teaching anyone.
2) **A** — Detail. The opening says Aunt Polly "had set him to whitewash the long board fence," high above his head, and he looks at it "with a sinking heart." No lost brush, unfriendly friends, or taken apple is mentioned.
3) **D** — Inference. Tom "refused" and said Aunt Polly was "so particular," making the chance seem rare and precious so Ben would want it more. It is part of his trick.
4) **C** — Vocabulary in context. To be "particular about" something is to be fussy and hard to please about it — not about money, removal, or the fence falling.
5) **A** — Tone. The passage describes Tom's clever trick and the boys being fooled in a playful, good-humoured way, with gentle irony — not sadness, fear, or anger.
