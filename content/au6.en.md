## Transcript

Have you ever wondered how bees turn flowers into the sweet honey on your toast?

It all starts when a worker bee flies from flower to flower, collecting a sugary liquid called nectar.

She stores the nectar in a special part of her body, sometimes called a 'honey stomach', and carries it back to the hive.

Back home, she passes the nectar to other bees, who add natural chemicals called enzymes that slowly change the sugars.

Then the bees spread the liquid across the honeycomb and fan it with their wings to dry it out.

This fanning removes most of the water, until the thin nectar becomes thick, sticky honey.

To fill just one small jar, bees may have to visit more than a million flowers.

When the honey is ready, the bees seal each cell with a thin cap of wax to keep it fresh for the winter.

Because honey has so little water and is naturally acidic, it almost never goes bad.

In fact, jars of honey found in ancient tombs were still good to eat thousands of years later.

So the next time you taste honey, remember it is really the hard work of thousands of tiny bees.
