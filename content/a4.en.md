## Passage

*— Adapted from "The Story of an Hour" by Kate Chopin (1894). Public domain.*

Because Mrs. Mallard had a weak heart, everyone was careful about how they told her the news. Her sister Josephine spoke in soft, broken sentences; her husband's friend Richards stood close by. There had been a railway accident, they said, and Brently Mallard's name was at the top of the list of the dead.

Some women would have refused to believe it. Mrs. Mallard did not. She wept at once, suddenly and wildly, in her sister's arms. Then, when the first storm of grief had passed, she went upstairs to her room alone and asked to be left there.

A comfortable armchair faced an open window. She sank into it, tired to the bone. Outside, the tops of the trees trembled with new spring life. The air smelled of coming rain, and somewhere below a bird was singing.

She sat almost still, but something was creeping toward her — she could feel it, though she could not yet name it. It reached her at last. Under her breath, again and again, she whispered one word: "Free, free, free!" The fear left her eyes, which now looked bright and alert.

She knew she would weep again when she saw her husband's kind hands folded in death. But beyond that moment she saw a long line of years that would belong to her, and to her alone. She opened her arms wide to welcome them.

## Notes

- **Source / author:** Kate Chopin (1850–1904) was an early figure in American feminist writing. "The Story of an Hour" (1894) is very short, yet it turns a whole inner life around in a single hour, and is now read as a classic of early female self-awareness.
- **Main idea:** The story shows how marriage — even a kind one — could quietly limit a woman's sense of self. The shocking news of her husband's death is what first lets Mrs. Mallard feel "free," revealing her deep wish for a life of her own.
- **Structure / craft:** The bright spring scene outside the window (new leaves, coming rain, birdsong) stands for new life and hope and contrasts with the mourning indoors. The slow approach of "something" builds suspense as her feeling changes.
- **Tone:** The narration is calm and controlled on the surface, but full of hidden movement, shifting from grief to a secret, growing sense of release.
- **Note:** This passage is an original retelling adapted from Chopin's public-domain story for B1–B2 learners; it is not the original text.

## Reading Comprehension

1. As she sits by the open window, what does Mrs. Mallard mainly begin to feel?
- A) Growing anger at Josephine and Richards for lying to her.
- B) An unexpected sense of freedom about the years ahead.
- C) A wish to join her husband in death.
- D) Regret that she had not loved her husband more.

2. Why were the others so careful in the way they told Mrs. Mallard the news?
- A) They were not sure the report was true.
- B) She had a weak heart.
- C) She had quarrelled with her sister Josephine.
- D) She had been ill in bed for many years.

3. What can we best infer about Mrs. Mallard's marriage from the passage?
- A) Her husband had treated her cruelly and openly.
- B) She had never loved her husband at all.
- C) Even a kind marriage had limited her sense of freedom.
- D) She had already been planning to leave her husband.

4. In the passage, "she whispered one word under her breath" means she said it
- A) in a loud, angry voice.
- B) very quietly.
- C) while she was fast asleep.
- D) directly to her sister Josephine.

5. What is the main effect of describing the spring trees, the coming rain and the singing bird outside the window?
- A) They warn the reader that a dangerous storm is coming.
- B) They show how cold and empty the season feels to her.
- C) They contrast with the mourning indoors and suggest new life.
- D) They prove that the news of the death was false.

**Answer Key**

1) **B** — Main idea. Sitting by the window, she feels "something" approach, finally whispers "Free, free, free!" and sees years "that would belong to her, and to her alone." This is an unexpected sense of freedom about the future.
2) **B** — Detail. The opening states plainly that "because Mrs. Mallard had a weak heart, everyone was careful about how they told her the news."
3) **C** — Inference. She knows she will still weep at her husband's "kind hands," so he was not cruel; yet she feels free and longs for years "to her alone." We can infer that even a kind marriage had limited her freedom. A, B and D have no support in the text.
4) **B** — Vocabulary in context. "Under one's breath" means very quietly, in a whisper; the following line, "the fear left her eyes," also shows this is private, controlled speech.
5) **C** — Tone / craft. The new leaves, coming rain and birdsong stand for new life and hope and contrast with the mourning inside, hinting at her reawakening — not warning of a storm or proving the news false.
