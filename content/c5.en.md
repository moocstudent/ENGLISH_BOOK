## Passage

*— Adapted from The Legend of Sleepy Hollow, Washington Irving (1820). A public-domain work.*

Among the wide hills beside a great river lies a small valley, one of the quietest places in the whole country. A little brook flows through it with a soft, sleepy murmur, and the loudest sound is usually the whistle of a bird or the far-off call of a crow. Years ago I once went shooting there, and I still remember the deep stillness that seemed to fill the air.

The people of the valley say that a strange power hangs over the land. Some believe the place was charmed long ago, so that a dreamy spell now lies upon the minds of all who live there. They walk slowly, speak softly, and love to tell old tales. They see visions, hear music in the empty air, and often fall into long daydreams.

But the most famous story of all is that of the Headless Horseman. Long ago, they say, a soldier lost his head to a cannonball in a forgotten battle, and now his ghost rides out each night in search of it. On dark evenings he gallops along the lonely roads, his black horse breathing hard, hurrying to reach the old churchyard before morning. Travellers fear most the wooden bridge among the trees, for it is there, the people whisper, that the silent rider is most often seen.

## Vocabulary

| Word / Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| brook | a small stream |
| murmur | a low, soft, continuous sound |
| stillness | complete quiet; the absence of noise |
| hang over | to fill or cover a place (as a feeling does) |
| charmed | placed under a magic spell |
| spell | a magic influence or power |
| visions | things seen that are not really there |
| daydream | a pleasant dreaming while awake |
| headless | having no head |
| gallop | (of a horse) to run very fast |
| churchyard | the ground around a church, used for graves |
| whisper | to speak very softly and secretly |

## Notes

- **Source / author:** *The Legend of Sleepy Hollow* (1820) by the American writer Washington Irving is an early classic of American gothic and folk legend. This passage is a simplified adaptation of the story's opening, in the writer's own words — not the original text.
- **Main idea:** A quiet valley seems to lie under a "sleepy spell," and out of this dreamy calm rises the region's most famous tale — the Headless Horseman, the ghost of a dead soldier.
- **Structure / craft:** The writer first builds deep quiet (the brook, the birds, the stillness), then the dreamy habits of the people, and only then the legend — layer by layer. Phrases like "they say" and "the people whisper" give the folk tale its half-believed, suspenseful feel.
- **Tone:** Slow and dreamlike, yet touched with mystery and a mild sense of fear.

## Reading Comprehension

1. What is the main quality of the valley described in the passage?
- A) Its busy markets and noisy streets
- B) Its rich farms and large towns
- C) Its cold and stormy weather
- D) Its deep quiet and dreamy stillness

2. According to the legend, how did the soldier lose his head?
- A) To a cannonball in a battle
- B) In a fall from his horse
- C) In a fire at the churchyard
- D) The story does not say

3. Why, most likely, do the people "see visions" and "fall into long daydreams"?
- A) Because a dreamy spell is said to lie over the valley
- B) Because they are often ill
- C) Because they never sleep at night
- D) Because they drink from the brook

4. In "a soft, sleepy murmur," the word *murmur* most nearly means
- A) a loud crash
- B) a low, gentle sound
- C) a bright light
- D) a sudden silence

5. Why do travellers fear the wooden bridge most of all?
- A) It is old and dangerous to cross.
- B) It is very far from the village.
- C) It is where the silent rider is most often seen.
- D) It is too narrow for a horse.

**Answer Key**

1) **D** — Main idea. The valley is called "one of the quietest places in the whole country," with a "sleepy" brook and "deep stillness"; a "dreamy spell" is said to lie over it. There is no mention of busy markets, storms, or large towns.
2) **A** — Detail. The passage states that the soldier "lost his head to a cannonball in a forgotten battle."
3) **A** — Inference. The text links the people's dreamy behaviour to the "dreamy spell" said to "lie upon the minds of all who live there." The other choices are never suggested as causes.
4) **B** — Vocabulary in context. The brook flows "with a soft, sleepy murmur," and the passage says the loudest sound is only a bird's whistle; so a *murmur* is a low, gentle sound, the opposite of "a loud crash."
5) **C** — Detail / craft. The passage explains that on the bridge "the silent rider is most often seen," which is why travellers "fear most" that spot. This detail fixes the haunting to one place and adds suspense.
