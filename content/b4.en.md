## Passage

*— Adapted from Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, Lewis Carroll (1865). A public-domain work.*

Alice was beginning to feel very tired of sitting beside her sister on the riverbank with nothing whatever to do. Once or twice she had peeped into the book her sister was reading, but it held no pictures and no conversations, and Alice could not see the point of such a book. The afternoon was warm, and her thoughts were growing slow and sleepy.

Then, all at once, a White Rabbit with pink eyes hurried past her through the grass. There was nothing very surprising in that, and Alice was hardly startled even when the Rabbit muttered to itself, "Oh dear! Oh dear! I shall be late!" But when it drew a watch out of the pocket of its little waistcoat, glanced at the time, and rushed on, she sprang to her feet in astonishment. Never in her life had she seen a rabbit wearing a waistcoat, still less one carrying a watch to look at.

Filled with curiosity, she raced across the field after it, and reached the hedge just in time to see the Rabbit disappear down a large hole beneath it. Without a single thought of how she might ever climb out again, Alice hurried straight in after him.

The passage ran on like a tunnel and then, quite suddenly, fell away beneath her feet. Down, down she went, so gently and so slowly that she had time to gaze about her at the shelves and cupboards that lined the walls of that strange, deep well.

## Vocabulary

| Word / Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| be tired of | to feel bored with something |
| peep into | to look quickly and secretly into |
| mutter | to speak in a low, unclear voice to oneself |
| waistcoat | a short, sleeveless garment worn over a shirt (a vest) |
| glance at | to look at quickly |
| spring to one's feet | to jump up suddenly |
| astonishment | great surprise |
| filled with curiosity | very eager to know or see something |
| hedge | a line of bushes forming a boundary |
| fall away | (of ground) to drop down suddenly |
| gaze about | to look around slowly and steadily |
| deep well | a deep hole falling far into the ground |

## Notes

- **Source / author:** *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* (1865) is the nonsense classic of the English writer Lewis Carroll (the pen name of the mathematician Charles Dodgson). This passage is an adaptation of the novel's opening, not the original text.
- **Main idea:** Curiosity turns a dull afternoon into the doorway to Wonderland: a talking rabbit in a waistcoat, checking a watch, leads Alice down the rabbit-hole.
- **Structure / craft:** The scene moves in order — boredom, the rabbit appears, the chase, the fall. Impossible details (a waistcoat, a pocket-watch) push the ordinary toward the absurd, and the slow fall creates a dreamlike rhythm.
- **Tone:** Light and playful, full of a child's wonder; the calm telling makes the strange feel "curious but fun."

## Reading Comprehension

1. Which statement best describes what happens in this passage?
- A) Alice reads an exciting book beside the river.
- B) Alice's curiosity leads her to follow a strange rabbit underground.
- C) Alice loses her sister in a deep forest.
- D) Alice falls asleep and dreams of school.

2. Why was Alice not interested in her sister's book?
- A) It was written in a foreign language.
- B) It was far too long to finish.
- C) It had no pictures and no conversations.
- D) Her sister would not let her read it.

3. What can the reader infer from Alice's reaction to the Rabbit?
- A) She was used to seeing talking animals every day.
- B) A rabbit with a waistcoat and a watch was something impossible and new to her.
- C) She was afraid the Rabbit would hurt her.
- D) She thought the Rabbit was her own lost pet.

4. In "she sprang to her feet in astonishment," the word *sprang* most nearly means
- A) jumped up quickly
- B) fell down slowly
- C) turned around calmly
- D) walked away quietly

5. The way Alice falls "so gently and so slowly" mainly helps to
- A) show that she is in great danger
- B) create a calm, dreamlike mood as the strange world begins
- C) prove that the well is not very deep
- D) explain how rabbits dig their holes

**Answer Key**

1) **B** — Main idea. The whole passage shows Alice's curiosity leading her to follow a strange rabbit underground into a new world. A, C and D do not match the events.
2) **C** — Detail. The text says the book "held no pictures and no conversations," which is why Alice found it dull.
3) **B** — Inference. She had never in her life seen a rabbit wearing a waistcoat; a rabbit with a waistcoat and a watch is impossible in the real world, and that impossibility is exactly what sparks her curiosity — not A, C or D.
4) **A** — Vocabulary in context. To "spring to one's feet" is to jump up suddenly, here out of astonishment, so *sprang* means jumped up quickly.
5) **B** — Craft / tone. The slow, gentle fall gives Alice time to look around and creates a calm, dreamlike mood that signals the start of the strange world — not danger (A) or a shallow well (C).
