## Core Notes

### Politeness, briefly

Linguists explain politeness through **face**: we all want to be respected (positive face) and left unimposed on (negative face). In English, **indirect = polite** — the bigger the imposition, the softer the words.

- **Hedging:** *maybe, a bit, sort of, I was wondering if…* leave the other person room.
- **Give an out:** *if that's okay / if you have time / no worries if not.*
- Bigger request → longer, more indirect wording.

### Indirect requests and refusals

English requests sit on a directness scale; the harder the ask, the further right you go:

| Directness | Example | Setting |
|---|---|---|
| Most direct | *Open the window.* | Urgent, command |
| Neutral | *Can you open the window?* | Everyday, friends |
| Polite | *Could you open the window?* | General politeness |
| Very polite | *Would you mind opening the window?* | Boss or stranger |

**Refuse "soft then no":** thank or agree first, then decline with a short reason.

- *Thanks for thinking of me, but I can't make it this time.*
- *I'd love to, but I'm swamped this week.*
- A bare *No.* often sounds blunt in English.

### Humour and sarcasm risks

- **Self-deprecation** is common and warm, but overdone it can suggest low confidence.
- **Sarcasm** (saying the opposite: *Oh, great, another meeting.*) works with close friends but misfires across cultures.
- If you're unsure someone will get it, don't use it — and never joke about looks, accent, religion, or body.
- **British understatement:** *not bad* often means "very good"; *a bit of a problem* may mean a big one.

### Body language & paralanguage

- **Eye contact:** moderate contact reads as sincere; avoiding it entirely can look shifty.
- **Distance:** English-speaking cultures keep more personal space.
- **Gestures** can mean different things elsewhere — use them carefully.
- **Paralanguage:** tone, volume, pace, pauses; a rising tone softens, a falling tone sounds sure.
- **Silence** can feel awkward in English talk and is often filled quickly.

### Case discussion

- **"Your English is so good!"** — usually kind, but can feel othering. A light reply: *Thanks — still working on it!*
- **"We should grab lunch sometime."** — often phatic, not a real invite; don't reach for your calendar.
- **Declining food:** *I don't want it* is harsh — try *It looks delicious, but I'm really full — thank you.*
- **Observe–adjust–ask:** watch how people request, refuse, and joke; adjust your directness; when unsure, ask politely (*How do people usually do this here?*).

### Summary

Politeness protects face; bigger asks go more indirect. Refuse soft-then-no with a short reason. Sarcasm travels badly — leave it out when unsure. Eyes, distance, and tone carry meaning; use observe–adjust–ask.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1.** Make it indirect: *Send me the file.* → **Would you mind sending me the file when you get a chance?**

**Example 2.** Decline gracefully (weekend overtime).

> **Answer.** *Thanks for asking, but I've already got plans this weekend. Maybe I can help on Monday instead?*

**Example 3.** Read between the lines: a British colleague says *It's not bad at all.*

> **Answer.** It's **praise** (understatement) — it means "quite good," not "barely passable."

**Example 4.** Fix (too blunt): *No. I don't like your idea.*

> **Answer.** *I see your point, but I'm not sure it fits our budget — could we look at another option?*

**Example 5.** Reflection fragment.

> **Answer.** *At my new job, colleagues rarely say a flat "no." They say "I'll see what I can do." At first it felt vague; now I read it as a polite way to buy time.*

## Exercises

1. Which concept do linguists use to explain politeness?
2. Which request form suits a boss or stranger?
3. What does British *not bad* usually mean?
4. Soften: *No, I won't come.*
5. Why be careful with sarcasm around people you don't know well?
6. Name the three-step habit for cross-cultural sensitivity.

**Answer key.** 1) Face. 2) *Would you mind + -ing?* / *Could you…?* 3) "Quite good" (understated praise). 4) e.g. *Thanks for the invite, but I won't be able to make it.* 5) Sarcasm relies on reversed meaning; without shared tone or culture it's easily misread or offensive. 6) Observe–adjust–ask.
