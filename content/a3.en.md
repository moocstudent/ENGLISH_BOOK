## Passage

*— "The Happy Prince", Oscar Wilde (1888). Public domain.*

High above the city, on a tall column, stood the statue of the Happy Prince. He was gilded all over with thin leaves of fine gold, for eyes he had two bright sapphires, and a large red ruby glowed on his sword-hilt. He was very much admired indeed. …

One night there flew over the city a little Swallow. His friends had gone away to Egypt six weeks before, but he had stayed behind, for he was in love with the most beautiful Reed. … At night-time he arrived at the city. … Then he saw the statue on the tall column. "I will put up there," he cried; "it is a fine position, with plenty of fresh air." So he alighted just between the feet of the Happy Prince.

… just as he was putting his head under his wing a large drop of water fell on him. … A third drop fell, and he looked up, and saw—Ah! what did he see?

The eyes of the Happy Prince were filled with tears, and tears were running down his golden cheeks. … "Who are you?" he said.

"I am the Happy Prince."

"Why are you weeping then?" asked the Swallow.

"When I was alive and had a human heart," answered the statue, "I lived in the Palace of Sans-Souci, where sorrow is not allowed to enter. … And now that I am dead they have set me up here so high that I can see all the ugliness and all the misery of my city, and though my heart is made of lead yet I cannot chose but weep."

… "Far away in a little street there is a poor house. One of the windows is open, and through it I can see a woman seated at a table. Her face is thin and worn, and she has coarse, red hands, all pricked by the needle, for she is a seamstress. In a bed in the corner of the room her little boy is lying ill. He has a fever, and is asking for oranges. His mother has nothing to give him but river water, so he is crying. Swallow, Swallow, little Swallow, will you not bring her the ruby out of my sword-hilt? My feet are fastened to this pedestal and I cannot move."

## Notes

- **Genre / source:** A literary fairy tale — the title opening of Oscar Wilde's collection *The Happy Prince and Other Tales* (1888).
- **Main idea:** A splendid, widely admired statue called "happy" looks down on human suffering, weeps, and begins to give his riches away — opening the tale's theme of compassion and self-sacrifice.
- **Structure / craft:** Personification (a statue that weeps) and symbolism (gold and jewels = a beautiful surface; a heart of lead = real sorrow and pity), set against a sharp contrast between the city's praise of the "happy" prince and his tears.
- **Tone:** Aesthetic and lyrical, touched with sadness and irony — a figure named "happy" who weeps at what he sees.

## Reading Comprehension

1. Which statement best captures the main idea of this opening?
- A) A proud statue enjoys being admired by the whole city.
- B) A little Swallow decides to give up his journey to Egypt.
- C) Though splendid and called "happy," the statue weeps at the misery it now sees and wants to help.
- D) A poor seamstress asks a statue for money.

2. Why had the little Swallow stayed behind when his friends flew to Egypt?
- A) He was too tired to make the long journey.
- B) He had fallen in love with a Reed.
- C) He wanted to guard the Happy Prince.
- D) The weather was too cold for flying.

3. Why can the Prince now see "all the ugliness and all the misery" of the city, which he did not know before?
- A) His sapphire eyes give him magical sight.
- B) The Swallow has described the city to him.
- C) In life he was shut inside a palace where sorrow could not enter, but now he stands high above the whole city.
- D) The city has become much poorer since he died.

4. In "he alighted just between the feet of the Happy Prince," the word *alighted* most nearly means
- A) landed
- B) climbed
- C) sang
- D) melted

5. The statue is called the "Happy Prince," yet it weeps. This contrast mainly works to
- A) suggest the Prince has lost his memory
- B) show that the Swallow is dreaming
- C) prove that the statue is broken
- D) create irony and hint at the story's theme of hidden sorrow and compassion

**Answer Key**

1) **C** — Main idea. The opening first shows the statue as splendid and "very much admired," then reveals "tears were running down his golden cheeks." He weeps over "all the ugliness and all the misery" and asks the Swallow to carry his ruby to the poor. The gist: a seemingly happy statue weeps at suffering and moves to help.
2) **B** — Detail. The text says: "he had stayed behind, for he was in love with the most beautiful Reed."
3) **C** — Inference. The Prince says that in life he "lived in the Palace of Sans-Souci, where sorrow is not allowed to enter," but now they "have set me up here so high that I can see all the ugliness and all the misery." Walled off from suffering in life, he can now see it from his height.
4) **A** — Vocabulary in context. After flying, "he alighted just between the feet of the Happy Prince" and then prepared to sleep, so *alighted* means came down and settled — i.e. landed.
5) **D** — Tone / craft. A statue named the "Happy Prince" that is "weeping" sets name against reality — irony — signalling the tale's theme: real sorrow and compassion beneath a beautiful, "happy" surface.
