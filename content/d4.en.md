## Passage

*— Adapted from Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, Frederick Douglass (1845). A public-domain work.*

When I first came to live in Baltimore, my new mistress was a kind woman who had never owned a slave before. She did not yet know the rules of slavery. Seeing that I wanted to learn, she began to teach me the letters of the alphabet, and soon I could spell short words. I felt proud, and she seemed pleased with what I had done.

But when her husband found out, he was angry and told her to stop at once. "Teach him to read," he warned her, "and he will no longer be fit to be a slave." Learning, he said, would spoil me, make me discontented, and put thoughts of freedom into my head. A slave, he believed, should know nothing but the orders of his master.

His words were meant to keep me down, yet they had the opposite effect. For the first time I saw the truth clearly. The very thing my master feared was the very thing I most needed. Reading was the road that led from slavery toward freedom. If knowledge was so dangerous to him, it must be precious to me.

From that day I made a silent promise. Whatever it might cost, I would learn to read. My mistress could teach me no more, and in time slavery hardened her too. So I found other teachers — poor white boys I met in the street — and, letter by letter, I began the long journey toward freedom.

## Vocabulary

| Word / Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| mistress | the female head of a household; here, a female slave-owner |
| the alphabet | the set of letters used in a language |
| spell | to name or write the letters of a word in order |
| find out | to discover; to learn a fact |
| fit to be a slave | suitable or good enough to remain a slave |
| spoil | to ruin someone's character; to make worse |
| discontented | not satisfied; unhappy with one's situation |
| keep (someone) down | to hold someone in a low, powerless position |
| the opposite effect | a result that is the reverse of what was intended |
| precious | very valuable; greatly to be treasured |
| harden | to become cold or unfeeling |
| letter by letter | one letter at a time; very gradually |

## Notes

- **Source / author:** *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass* (1845) is the autobiography of Frederick Douglass, a formerly enslaved man who became a leader of the American anti-slavery movement. It is told in the first person. This passage is an adaptation in simpler English, not the original text.
- **Main idea:** Literacy is the key that can free an enslaved person; the master's very effort to forbid reading proves how powerful knowledge is.
- **Structure / craft:** The passage moves in order — learning letters, the master's ban, a sudden understanding, a private vow. The master's warning and the narrator's insight form an irony: the thing his master fears most becomes the narrator's guiding light.
- **Tone:** Looking back with quiet strength; the mood turns from a child's simple pride to a clear, determined longing for freedom.

## Reading Comprehension

1. What is the main idea of the passage?
- A) A kind mistress simply enjoyed teaching a boy to spell.
- B) Slaves in the city of Baltimore were treated very well.
- C) Learning to read became the narrator's path toward freedom.
- D) Reading was too difficult for the narrator ever to learn.

2. Who first began to teach the narrator the alphabet?
- A) His master
- B) His mistress
- C) Poor white boys in the street
- D) Another enslaved man

3. Why did the master order his wife to stop the lessons?
- A) He thought the boy was too young to learn.
- B) He feared that an educated slave would want freedom and be hard to control.
- C) He wanted to teach the boy himself.
- D) He believed reading was simply a waste of the boy's time.

4. In "he will no longer be fit to be a slave," the word *fit* most nearly means
- A) suitable
- B) healthy
- C) ready
- D) strong

5. How does the master's warning affect the narrator?
- A) It frightens him into giving up the idea of reading.
- B) It convinces him that reading is dangerous and useless.
- C) It makes him feel grateful and loyal to his master.
- D) It has the opposite effect and strengthens his desire to learn.

**Answer Key**

1) **C** — Main idea. The heart of the passage is the narrator's realization that "reading was the road that led from slavery toward freedom," followed by his vow to teach himself. A and C are minor or untrue, and D is contradicted by "letter by letter, I began the long journey toward freedom."
2) **B** — Detail. The text says his mistress "began to teach me the letters of the alphabet." The white boys came later, and the master forbade the lessons.
3) **B** — Inference. The master warns that a boy who can read "will no longer be fit to be a slave" and says learning would make him discontented and full of thoughts of freedom — that is, an educated slave would seek freedom and be hard to control.
4) **A** — Vocabulary in context. "Fit to be a slave" means suitable or good enough to remain one; here *fit* means suitable, not healthy or strong.
5) **D** — Tone / craft. The text says the words "were meant to keep me down, yet they had the opposite effect" — instead of crushing him, they strengthened his desire to learn.
