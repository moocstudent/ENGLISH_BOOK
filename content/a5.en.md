## Passage

*— Adapted from "The Necklace" by Guy de Maupassant (1884). Public domain.*

Mathilde was pretty and charming, but she had been born into a poor family and married only a modest clerk. All day she dreamed of elegant rooms, fine dresses, and rich admirers, and her plain little home made her miserable.

One evening her husband proudly brought home an invitation to a grand ball. Instead of being pleased, Mathilde cried, for she had nothing suitable to wear. Her husband gave up the money he had been saving for a gun so that she could buy a dress. Even then she was unhappy, because she owned no jewels. So she went to her wealthy friend, Madame Forestier, who kindly lent her a brilliant diamond necklace.

At the ball Mathilde was the most beautiful woman in the room, and everyone admired her. But when she reached home in the early morning, the necklace was gone from her neck. They searched everywhere and found nothing. Too ashamed to confess, the couple borrowed a fortune, bought a matching necklace, and returned it to Madame Forestier, who noticed nothing.

For ten long years they lived in poverty, working hard and saving every coin to pay back the terrible debt. Mathilde grew old, rough, and tired.

One day she met Madame Forestier and told her the whole story at last. Her friend, deeply moved, took her hands. "Oh, my poor Mathilde," she said. "My necklace was not real. It was worth almost nothing."

## Notes

- **Source / author:** Guy de Maupassant (1850–1893) was a French master of the short story, known for tight realism and surprise endings. "The Necklace" (1884) is one of his most famous tales.
- **Main idea:** The story criticises vanity and the wish to seem richer than one is. Mathilde trades ten years of her life for one glittering evening — and the whole disaster rests on a necklace that was fake.
- **Structure / craft:** The lost necklace is quietly set up early, then the ending turns everything around with the reveal that it "was worth almost nothing." Her admired night at the ball stands in sharp, ironic contrast with the ten years of hard labour that follow.
- **Tone:** The narration is calm and matter-of-fact, with a touch of pity and quiet mockery; the flat final line lands the heavy irony of fate.
- **Note:** This passage is an original retelling adapted from Maupassant's public-domain story for B1–B2 learners; it is not the original text.

## Reading Comprehension

1. What is the main point the story makes through Mathilde's life?
- A) Vanity and pride can cost far more than they are worth.
- B) Hard work always leads to great wealth in the end.
- C) True friends should never lend valuable things.
- D) City life is always better than life in the countryside.

2. How did Mathilde get the necklace she wore to the ball?
- A) She borrowed it from her friend Madame Forestier.
- B) She had owned it since she was a child.
- C) Her husband bought it with his savings.
- D) She bought it with money she borrowed from a bank.

3. Why did the couple fall into debt for ten long years?
- A) They kept spending money on more and more balls.
- B) The husband lost his job as a clerk.
- C) They secretly bought a real necklace to replace the lost one.
- D) Madame Forestier demanded a large payment from them.

4. In the passage, "Too ashamed to confess" means they were too ashamed to
- A) pay the money back.
- B) buy Mathilde a new dress.
- C) attend the grand ball.
- D) admit what had happened.

5. The final lines, in which Madame Forestier says the necklace "was worth almost nothing," mainly create
- A) a happy ending in which Mathilde gets her money back.
- B) an ironic twist that makes the ten years seem tragically wasted.
- C) a warning that Madame Forestier is a dishonest woman.
- D) a lesson about the correct way to store expensive jewels.

**Answer Key**

1) **A** — Main idea. Mathilde and her husband carry a huge debt for ten years for one glamorous night, only to learn the necklace was fake — showing the heavy cost of vanity and pride.
2) **A** — Detail. The text says she went to her wealthy friend, Madame Forestier, "who kindly lent her a brilliant diamond necklace."
3) **C** — Inference. After losing the necklace and being "too ashamed to confess," the couple "borrowed a fortune" and "bought a matching necklace," which is why they owed money for ten years.
4) **D** — Vocabulary in context. To "confess" is to admit something; being "too ashamed to confess" means they could not bring themselves to admit that the necklace was lost.
5) **B** — Tone / craft. The reveal that the necklace "was worth almost nothing" clashes with the ten years of labour, forming a classic ironic twist that makes their sacrifice feel tragically wasted — not a happy ending or a moral lesson about jewels.
