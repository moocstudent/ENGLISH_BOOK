## Passage

*— Adapted from Pride and Prejudice, Jane Austen (1813). A public-domain work.*

"It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man with a large fortune must be in want of a wife." Whether the young man wishes it or not, the families around him treat him at once as the rightful property of one of their daughters.

So it was in the Bennet household. One morning Mrs Bennet hurried to her husband with thrilling news: Netherfield Park, the great house nearby, had been rented at last. The new tenant, she said, was a rich young gentleman from the north of England — a Mr Bingley, single, with four or five thousand pounds a year. What a fine thing, she declared, for their five daughters!

Mr Bennet asked, calmly, how this could possibly concern the girls. His wife was astonished at him. Could he not see that Mr Bingley might marry one of them? He must therefore call on the young man the very moment he arrived.

Her husband, who dearly loved to tease her, pretended not to follow. Perhaps, he suggested, Mrs Bennet and the girls should go alone; or he might send a short letter, giving his consent to whichever daughter Mr Bingley liked best. His wife cried that he took no notice of her poor nerves. On the contrary, he replied gently, he had the greatest respect for her nerves — they had been his faithful companions for twenty years.

## Vocabulary

| Word / Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| universally acknowledged | accepted as true by everyone |
| in want of | needing; lacking |
| fortune | a large amount of money or wealth |
| tenant | a person who rents and lives in a place |
| rent (a house) | to pay to use a house that belongs to someone else |
| concern | to be about or involve someone |
| astonished | very surprised |
| call on (someone) | to visit someone |
| consent | permission; agreement |
| take no notice of | to pay no attention to |
| tease | to make gentle fun of someone |
| nerves | nervous, anxious feelings (here, Mrs Bennet's excitable state) |

## Notes

- **Source / author:** *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) is the best-known novel of the English writer Jane Austen, famous for its sharp social observation and witty dialogue. This passage is an adaptation of the opening, not the original text.
- **Main idea:** The ironic first sentence shows how this society automatically treated a wealthy single man as a target for unmarried daughters — marriage and money are tightly linked.
- **Structure / craft:** A witty, proverb-like opening line leads into the Bennets' conversation; the characters are drawn through dialogue, contrasting the mother's eagerness with the father's calm.
- **Tone:** Playful and ironic; the narrator sounds perfectly serious while quietly mocking.

## Reading Comprehension

1. The famous first sentence of the passage is best described as
- A) a serious scientific fact
- B) a playful, ironic remark about society
- C) an angry complaint about rich men
- D) a sad reflection on lost love

2. What is the exciting news that Mrs Bennet brings to her husband?
- A) One of their daughters is getting married.
- B) A wealthy single man has rented Netherfield Park.
- C) Mr Bennet has been left a large fortune.
- D) Mr Bingley has invited the family to dinner.

3. Why does Mrs Bennet want her husband to visit Mr Bingley?
- A) To borrow money from him
- B) To rent Netherfield Park for themselves
- C) So that Mr Bingley might marry one of their daughters
- D) To warn him about the neighbourhood

4. In the passage, "in want of a wife" means the man
- A) refuses to marry
- B) needs or lacks a wife
- C) already has a wife
- D) dislikes women

5. How does Mr Bennet mainly react to his wife's excitement?
- A) He shares her excitement completely.
- B) He becomes angry and refuses to speak.
- C) He gently teases her and pretends not to understand.
- D) He rushes off at once to visit Mr Bingley.

**Answer Key**

1) **B** — Main idea / tone. The line announces "a single man ... must be in want of a wife" as a "truth universally acknowledged." This is irony — a joking comment on the society of the time, not a real fact, so A, C and D are wrong.
2) **B** — Detail. The text states that Netherfield Park "had been rented at last" by a rich, single young man, Mr Bingley. No marriage, inheritance or dinner invitation is mentioned.
3) **C** — Inference. Mrs Bennet is amazed her husband cannot see that "Mr Bingley might marry one of them," and insists he "call on the young man the very moment he arrived." She hopes for a match with a daughter.
4) **B** — Vocabulary in context. To "be in want of" something means to need or lack it, so the phrase means the man needs a wife.
5) **C** — Tone / craft. Mr Bennet "dearly loved to tease her" and "pretended not to follow," even joking about sending a letter of consent. He gently mocks her rather than agreeing, getting angry, or actually going to visit.
