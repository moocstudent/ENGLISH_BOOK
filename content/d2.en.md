## Passage

*— Adapted from Walden, Henry David Thoreau (1854). A public-domain work.*

I went to live in the woods because I wished to live on purpose. I wanted to face only the most important facts of life, and to see whether I could learn what it had to teach. I did not want to reach the end of my days and suddenly find that I had never truly lived. Life is precious, and I did not wish to waste it on things that were not really life. I wanted to live deeply and to know life fully.

Most people, it seems to me, live in a strange hurry. They fill their days with a hundred small tasks and worries, until their lives become as rough and troubled as a stormy sea. My own advice can be said in a single word: "Simplify, simplify." Instead of a thousand small affairs, keep only two or three. When life becomes simple, the mind grows calm and clear.

We work hard to build railways, send messages, and gather the news, yet we seldom stop to ask whether these things truly matter. We have almost become servants to the very machines we made. So I built a small cabin by the pond, grew my own beans, and lived on very little. There I learned that a person needs far less than he imagines. By giving up what was unnecessary, I made room for what was real: the morning light, the quiet water, and my own honest thoughts.

## Notes

- **Source / author:** *Walden* (1854) is the best-known work of the American writer and thinker Henry David Thoreau, a record of the two years he spent living simply in a cabin by Walden Pond. This passage is a simplified adaptation for learners, not the original text.
- **Main idea:** Thoreau went to the woods to live deliberately, to meet life's essential facts, and to avoid reaching death only to find he had never really lived — a call to simplicity.
- **Structure / craft:** A first-person reflection that moves from why he went, to a piece of advice ("Simplify, simplify"), to what simple living taught him; it leans on vivid images (a stormy sea, a cabin by the pond).
- **Tone:** Earnest, calm and quietly persuasive, gently critical of a hurried, cluttered modern life.

## Reading Comprehension

1. Why did the writer go to live in the woods?
- A) To become rich by farming
- B) To live deliberately and learn what life could teach
- C) To hide from a war
- D) To build a large new town

2. What does the writer compare the busy lives of most people to?
- A) A quiet garden
- B) A long, easy road
- C) A rough, stormy sea
- D) A warm fire

3. In the passage, "simplify" most nearly means
- A) to make something more exciting
- B) to make something less complicated
- C) to work much harder
- D) to travel far away

4. By saying "a person needs far less than he imagines," the writer suggests that
- A) people should buy more things
- B) farming is the only good life
- C) money brings happiness
- D) much of what people chase is unnecessary

5. What is the writer's attitude toward the hurried, complicated life of most people?
- A) He admires it and wants more of it
- B) He is confused by it
- C) He is critical of it and prefers simple living
- D) He does not care about it

**Answer Key**

1) **B** — Main idea. The writer went to the woods "to live on purpose," to "face only the most important facts of life" and "learn what it had to teach."
2) **C** — Detail. He compares most people's busy lives to something "as rough and troubled as a stormy sea."
3) **B** — Vocabulary in context. The next lines ("keep only two or three"; "the mind grows calm and clear") show that "simplify" means to make life less complicated.
4) **D** — Inference. Saying "a person needs far less than he imagines," and living on very little, suggests that much of what people chase is unnecessary.
5) **C** — Tone / craft. He criticises the "strange hurry" of modern life and urges the reader to "Simplify, simplify," so his attitude is critical and in favour of simple living.
