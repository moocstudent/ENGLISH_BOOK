## Passage

*— Adapted from "The Tell-Tale Heart" by Edgar Allan Poe (1843). Public domain.*

He swears that he is not mad. True, he admits, he is nervous — very, very nervous — but why, he asks, should that mean he is insane? "Listen," he says, "and see how calmly I can tell the whole story."

A madman, he insists, could never plan so carefully or speak so clearly. He had nothing against the old man he lived with; he even loved him. The old man had never wronged him or spoken a hard word. There was only one thing the narrator could not bear — the old man's eye. It was a pale blue eye, covered with a thin film, like the dull eye of a vulture. Whenever it turned toward him, his blood ran cold.

And so, he says, he made up his mind. For seven long nights he crept to the old man's door and watched him as he slept, moving with the patience of a hunter. Never had he been kinder to the old man than during that week — proof, he claims, of how clever and sane he was.

After the deed was done, the police arrived, for a neighbour had reported a cry in the night. The narrator welcomed them in, calm and smiling. But as they sat and talked, a low sound began to grow in his ears — a steady beating, louder and louder. It was, he was certain, the old man's heart. Could they not hear it too?

| bear (v.) | to stand or endure |

## Notes

- **Source / author:** Edgar Allan Poe (1809–1849) is a central figure in American gothic and horror writing. "The Tell-Tale Heart" (1843) is a famous short story told as a first-person monologue by a mind that is slowly breaking down.
- **Main idea:** The story is a study of guilt. The harder the narrator tries to prove he is sane, the more he reveals his madness; in the end the "beating heart" is really the echo of his own conscience, driving him to the edge.
- **Structure / craft:** It is a classic *unreliable narrator*. The whole text is his defence of himself, and the reader must see, between the lines, the truth that contradicts his self-image. Suspense and the growing sound of the "heart" build the tension step by step.
- **Tone:** Tense, excited and nervous; the narrator plays calm while giving himself away, which creates dramatic irony.
- **Note:** This passage is an original, mild retelling adapted from Poe's public-domain story for B1–B2 learners, with graphic detail removed; it is not the original text.

## Reading Comprehension

1. What is the passage mainly about?
- A) A calm man who clearly proves that he is perfectly sane.
- B) A guilty, disturbed narrator who insists he is not mad.
- C) A doctor carefully studying an old man's strange eye.
- D) A group of police officers solving a daring robbery.

2. What did the narrator most hate about the old man?
- A) His loud voice.
- B) His pale, film-covered eye.
- C) His hidden money.
- D) His cruel, hard words.

3. Why does the narrator keep repeating how calm and careful he is?
- A) He honestly feels no emotion of any kind at all.
- B) He hopes the police will give him a large reward.
- C) He is teaching the reader how to sleep more soundly.
- D) He is trying to prove he is not insane, though his story suggests otherwise.

4. In the passage, "his blood ran cold" means he felt
- A) sudden fear or horror.
- B) suddenly sleepy.
- C) ill with a high fever.
- D) warm and comfortable.

5. The steady beating that grows "louder and louder" at the end most likely represents
- A) a clock that is hidden under the floor.
- B) the police knocking hard at the door.
- C) the narrator's own guilt rising inside him.
- D) the old man slowly waking up again.

**Answer Key**

1) **B** — Main idea. The whole passage is the narrator's monologue trying to prove he is "not mad," yet it constantly leaks tension and guilt — the mark of an unreliable, disturbed narrator.
2) **B** — Detail. The text says the one thing he could not bear was the old man's "pale blue eye, covered with a thin film, like the dull eye of a vulture."
3) **D** — Inference. By insisting again and again how "clever and sane" and calm he is, he actually reveals the opposite: the harder he denies madness, the more disturbed he appears.
4) **A** — Vocabulary in context. "One's blood runs cold" is a fixed phrase meaning to feel sudden fear or horror; it describes his reaction to the eye.
5) **C** — Tone / craft. The ever-louder "beating" is not a real sound but the outward form of his guilt — the echo of his conscience pushing him toward collapse — which is the story's central use of symbolism.
