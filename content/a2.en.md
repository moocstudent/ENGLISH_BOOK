## Passage

*— "The Gift of the Magi", O. Henry (1905). Public domain.*

Jim drew a package from his overcoat pocket and threw it upon the table.

"Don't make any mistake, Dell," he said, "about me. I don't think there's anything in the way of a haircut or a shave or a shampoo that could make me like my girl any less. But if you'll unwrap that package you may see why you had me going a while at first."

White fingers and nimble tore at the string and paper. And then an ecstatic scream of joy; and then, alas! a quick feminine change to hysterical tears and wails …

For there lay The Combs—the set of combs, side and back, that Della had worshipped long in a Broadway window. Beautiful combs, pure tortoise shell, with jewelled rims—just the shade to wear in the beautiful vanished hair. … And now, they were hers, but the tresses that should have adorned the coveted adornments were gone.

But she hugged them to her bosom, and at length she was able to look up with dim eyes and a smile and say: "My hair grows so fast, Jim!"

… Jim had not yet seen his beautiful present. She held it out to him eagerly upon her open palm. … "Give me your watch. I want to see how it looks on it."

Instead of obeying, Jim tumbled down on the couch and put his hands under the back of his head and smiled.

"Dell," said he, "let's put our Christmas presents away and keep 'em a while. They're too nice to use just at present. I sold the watch to get the money to buy your combs. And now suppose you put the chops on."

The magi, as you know, were wise men—wonderfully wise men—who brought gifts to the Babe in the manger. They invented the art of giving Christmas presents. … And here I have lamely related to you the uneventful chronicle of two foolish children in a flat who most unwisely sacrificed for each other the greatest treasures of their house. But … let it be said that of all who give gifts these two were the wisest. … Everywhere they are wisest. They are the magi.

## Notes

- **Genre / source:** A short story by O. Henry (1905). This excerpt is the closing climax and the narrator's final comment.
- **Main idea:** True love is measured by the willingness to give up one's most prized possession for the other; though the gifts become materially useless, the sacrifice makes them priceless.
- **Structure / craft:** The famous "O. Henry ending" — a double twist: Della sold her hair to buy Jim a watch chain, while Jim sold his watch to buy Della's combs, so each gift is now unusable. The word "magi" frames the tale at both ends, lifting the ordinary couple to the level of the "wisest."
- **Tone:** Warm but ironic and gently humorous; the narrator steps in directly, first calling the pair "foolish children," then declaring them "the wisest" — a paradox that states the theme.

## Reading Comprehension

1. Which statement best expresses the main point of this ending?
- A) Jim was angry that Della had sold her beautiful hair.
- B) Each gave up their greatest treasure for the other, and that sacrifice made them truly wise.
- C) The couple learned never to waste money on expensive presents.
- D) The combs and the watch chain were both cheap and useless.

2. What had Jim done in order to buy Della's combs?
- A) He sold his watch.
- B) He saved eight dollars a week for a year.
- C) He borrowed money from a neighbour.
- D) He returned an earlier present to the shop.

3. Why does Della's joy turn so quickly to tears when she sees the combs?
- A) The combs are the wrong colour for her hair.
- B) She had sold her hair, so the combs are now useless to her.
- C) She had wanted a watch chain instead of combs.
- D) She thinks Jim spent far too much money.

4. In "the tresses that should have adorned the coveted adornments were gone," the word *coveted* most nearly means
- A) forgotten
- B) inexpensive
- C) borrowed
- D) strongly desired

5. The narrator calls Jim and Della "two foolish children" and yet "the wisest" of all gift-givers. This contrast is best described as
- A) a factual mistake by the narrator
- B) a sign that the narrator dislikes the couple
- C) deliberate irony that reveals the story's real meaning
- D) a simple description of their ages

**Answer Key**

1) **B** — Main idea. The narrator says the pair "most unwisely sacrificed for each other the greatest treasures of their house," then insists "of all who give gifts these two were the wisest... They are the magi": sacrificing your dearest thing for love is the truest wisdom. the other options and D contradict the text.
2) **A** — Detail. Jim states it directly: "I sold the watch to get the money to buy your combs."
3) **B** — Inference. The combs were "just the shade to wear in the beautiful vanished hair," but "the tresses... were gone." Della had sold her hair, so the longed-for combs are now useless — hence joy turning to tears.
4) **D** — Vocabulary in context. Della "had worshipped long" over these combs, and *coveted* sits beside *adorned/adornments* to mean deeply longed-for. So *coveted* ≈ strongly desired.
5) **C** — Tone / craft. Calling the self-sacrificing couple "foolish" and simultaneously "the wisest" is a deliberate paradox — verbal irony — that delivers the theme: a sacrifice the world might call foolish is in fact the highest wisdom of love.
