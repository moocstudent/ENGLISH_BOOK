## Passage

*— Adapted from The Voyage of the Beagle, Charles Darwin (1839). A public-domain work.*

Nothing in my life had prepared me for my first walk in a Brazilian forest. I had read about the tropics, but reading is a poor teacher of wonder. The moment I stepped beneath the trees, I was surrounded by a world so full of life that I hardly knew where to look first.

Great trees rose far above my head, their trunks bound together by thick, twisting vines. Flowers of every colour opened in the green shade, and the warm air was damp and heavy with sweet, strange smells. Broad leaves, larger than any I had seen, caught the little light that fell through the branches.

Everywhere there was movement and sound. Bright butterflies drifted past like flying flowers. Unfamiliar insects hummed and clicked in the undergrowth, and from the treetops came the calls of birds I could not name. Now and then a sudden cry rang out from deep in the forest, and then all was still again.

I walked slowly, my notebook in my hand, trying to write down what I saw. But the forest offered me far more than I could ever record. That night I wrote that, for a naturalist, such a day brought almost too much happiness; my mind was "a chaos of delight."

At last I sat upon a fallen trunk and simply looked and listened. The sheer variety of living things, packed so closely together, filled me with deep respect for the richness of nature. I felt that I had entered a world I would spend my life trying to understand.

## Notes

- **Source / author:** *The Voyage of the Beagle* (1839) is the travel journal of the English naturalist Charles Darwin, written during his round-the-world survey aboard HMS Beagle. His observations of tropical nature helped lead to his later theory of evolution. This passage is an adaptation for learners.
- **Main idea:** On his first walk in a Brazilian rainforest, the writer is overwhelmed by the variety and richness of life, full of wonder and respect.
- **Structure / craft:** A first-person description. It begins with not knowing "where to look first," then builds through sight (great trees, coloured flowers), sound (insects, birds) and smell (warm, damp air) — a fine example of sensory detail.
- **Tone:** Wondering, joyful and reverent, full of a naturalist's love of nature.

## Reading Comprehension

1. What is the main idea of the passage?
- A) The narrator is disappointed by the Brazilian forest.
- B) The narrator explains how to give names to tropical birds.
- C) The narrator is filled with wonder at the variety of life in the forest.
- D) The narrator prefers reading books to walking outdoors.

2. What was the narrator holding as he walked through the forest?
- A) A map
- B) A walking stick
- C) A butterfly net
- D) A notebook

3. What can we infer about the narrator from the passage?
- A) He is a sailor with no interest in plants or animals.
- B) He is lost and afraid in the forest.
- C) He is a painter searching for flowers to draw.
- D) He is a naturalist who studies living things.

4. In "the warm air was damp," the word *damp* most nearly means
- A) dry
- B) slightly wet
- C) fresh
- D) cold

5. How does the writer mainly bring the forest to life for the reader?
- A) Through details of sight, sound, and smell
- B) By listing the dates of his journey
- C) By giving exact scientific names for every species
- D) By comparing the forest to his home in England

**Answer Key**

1) **C** — Main idea. The passage keeps showing the writer's wonder at the forest's abundance: "a world so full of life," "almost too much happiness," "deep respect for the richness of nature." A and D are the opposite of his delight; C is a minor detail.
2) **D** — Detail. The text says, "my notebook in my hand, trying to write down what I saw."
3) **D** — Inference. He carries a notebook to record what he sees, calls such a day "for a naturalist... almost too much happiness," and admires the variety of species — so he is a naturalist who studies living things.
4) **B** — Vocabulary in context. *Damp* means slightly wet, fitting the "warm air... heavy with... smells" of the rainforest; it does not mean dry or cold.
5) **A** — Craft / tone. The writer uses details of sight (trees, flowers, butterflies), sound (humming insects, bird calls, a sudden cry) and smell (warm, damp air) to bring the forest alive; he does not list scientific names or dates, or compare it to England.
