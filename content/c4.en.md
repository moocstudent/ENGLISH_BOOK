## Passage

*— Adapted from Dracula, Bram Stoker (1897). A public-domain work.*

The coach left me alone at the Borgo Pass just as the last light died behind the mountains. The other travellers had watched me with frightened eyes, and one old woman had pressed a small cross into my hand before we parted. Now the road was empty, and the cold air smelled of pine and snow.

Then, out of the darkness, a black carriage appeared. Four dark horses drew it, and the driver sat wrapped in a long cloak, a great hat hiding his face. When he turned to me, I saw only his bright, hard eyes and his lips, which were strangely red. "You are early tonight, my friend," he said, and his voice was deep and cold. He lifted me into the carriage with a hand that gripped like iron.

We climbed higher into the hills. The horses ran without rest, and the wind grew sharp. Somewhere in the black forest a wolf began to howl, and then another, until the whole night seemed alive with the sound. The horses trembled, but the driver only laughed softly to himself.

At last, against the grey sky, I saw the castle. It was huge and broken, its tall towers black against the stars, and not a single light burned in any window. The carriage stopped. I stood in the silent yard, my heart beating fast, and understood that I could not now turn back.

## Notes

- **Source / author:** *Dracula* (1897) is the gothic-horror masterpiece of the Irish writer Bram Stoker. The novel is told through letters and journal entries (an epistolary form). This passage is an adaptation, in simpler modern English, of Jonathan Harker's journal as he travels to the castle — not the original text.
- **Main idea:** Night, cold wind, howling wolves and a ruined castle build the fear and unease of a man travelling alone, deeper and deeper, until he can no longer turn back.
- **Structure / craft:** A first-person journal that moves in order — left alone, the driver arrives, the night ride, the castle. Details of sight, sound and touch (red lips, an iron grip, howling wolves) hint that the driver is not an ordinary man.
- **Tone:** Tense and heavy, full of a sense that something is wrong; the telling stays calm even as the dread grows.

## Reading Comprehension

1. Which word best describes the overall mood of the passage?
- A) Cheerful and lively
- B) Calm and peaceful
- C) Dark and full of unease
- D) Busy and crowded

2. What had the old woman given the narrator before they parted?
- A) A warm coat
- B) A small cross
- C) A map of the road
- D) A burning lamp

3. What does the passage suggest about the driver?
- A) He is a frightened, ordinary servant.
- B) He is lost and does not know the road.
- C) He is strange and unafraid, unlike a normal man.
- D) He is an old friend the narrator has known for years.

4. In "the wind grew sharp," the word *sharp* most nearly means
- A) gentle
- B) bitterly cold
- C) loud
- D) sudden

5. How does the writer mainly create a feeling of dread?
- A) By describing bright colours and happy sounds
- B) By explaining the long history of the castle in detail
- C) Through darkness, howling wolves, and the silent ruined castle
- D) By listing the names of the other travellers

**Answer Key**

1) **C** — Main idea / mood. The dying light, the empty road, the strange driver, the howling wolves and the "huge and broken" castle with no lights all build a dark, uneasy feeling. A and D contradict the quiet, frightening setting; the scene is far from "peaceful," so B is wrong.
2) **B** — Detail. The text says the old woman "had pressed a small cross into my hand before we parted."
3) **C** — Inference. The driver has "strangely red" lips and a hand that "gripped like iron," and while the horses "trembled" at the wolves he "only laughed softly to himself." These details point to someone strange and unafraid, not an ordinary or frightened man.
4) **B** — Vocabulary in context. The wind grows "sharp" as they climb higher and the air turns colder, so *sharp* here means biting or painfully cold, not loud or sudden.
5) **C** — Craft / tone. The dread comes from setting and sound: the darkness, the wolves howling until "the whole night seemed alive," and the ruined castle with "not a single light." The writer does not use bright colours (A), a detailed history (B), or a list of names (D).
